    The six MCQ-testable science practice sub-skills, with stem patterns, annotated examples, and distractor strategies. Practice 1 (creating representations) is FRQ-only and is not shown. Per the 2024–25 revised CED, any learning objective may be tested with any sub-skill.

    2 Mathematical Routines — ~55–70% of MCQ section 2.A · 2.B · 2.C · 2.D
    Sub-skill Stem pattern & variants Annotated examples Distractor strategies
    2.A
    Derive a symbolic expression from known quantities

    MCQ: 15–20%
    Which of the following correctly expresses [unknown] in terms of [given variables]?
    • A student derives an expression for [X]. Which is correct?
    • Using only the quantities shown, which expression gives [X]?
    • Two students write different expressions for [X]. Which is valid?
    • [Scenario]. Which expression represents [X] in terms of [vars]?
    Ex 1 — Electric potential energy
    A point charge Q is fixed at the origin. A test charge q is placed distance r away. Which correctly expresses the electric potential energy U in terms of Q, q, r, and k?
    • (A) U = kQq / r
    • (B) U = kQq / r²
    • (C) U = kQ / r
    • (D) U = kQqr
    Why each fails: (B) is force, not energy. (C) drops q — that's potential V. (D) inverts r — targets students who misremember the proportionality.
    Ex 2 — Ideal gas (isothermal)
    A sealed gas at P₁, V₁ is isothermally compressed to P₂. Which correctly expresses V₂ in terms of P₁, V₁, and P₂?
    • (A) V₂ = P₂V₁ / P₁
    • (B) V₂ = P₁V₁ / P₂
    • (C) V₂ = P₁P₂ / V₁
    • (D) V₂ = V₁(P₁ + P₂)
    Why each fails: (A) inverts ratio. (C) wrong units. (D) replaces inverse proportion with addition — core Boyle's Law misconception.
    • Ratio inversionSwap numerator/denominator — extremely common, hard to spot fast.
    • Wrong-but-related equationF instead of U, V instead of U, E instead of F.
    • Missing variableDrop one variable — targets partial recall.
    • Wrong exponentr instead of r², v instead of v².
    • Arithmetic shortcutReplace multiplication with addition of variables.
    At least 2 of 3 wrong answers should be dimensionally consistent with the correct one. Dimensionally obvious distractors are too easy to eliminate.
    2.B
    Calculate or estimate an unknown quantity

    MCQ: 20–25%
    A [system] has [given values with units]. What is [unknown]?
    • What is the approximate value of [X]?
    • Which of the following is closest to [X]?
    • Based on the diagram/table, what is [X]?
    • What is the magnitude of [X]?
    Ex 1 — Series circuit
    A 12 V battery connects to R₁ = 4 Ω and R₂ = 8 Ω in series. What is the current through R₂?
    • (A) 0.75 A
    • (B) 1.5 A
    • (C) 1.0 A
    • (D) 3.0 A
    Why each fails: (A) uses R₁ only + half voltage. (B) uses R₁ as denominator alone. (D) uses R₂ alone. All three reflect the "use one resistor, not the sum" series misconception.
    Ex 2 — Double-slit interference
    λ = 600 nm, d = 0.30 mm, L = 1.5 m. What is the fringe spacing?
    • (A) 3.0 × 10⁻³ m
    • (B) 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m
    • (C) 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ m
    • (D) 2.7 × 10⁻³ m
    Why each fails: (B) inverts formula. (C) omits L. (D) uses λ in mm — unit conversion error. All four are same order of magnitude.
    • Unit conversion errornm instead of m, cm instead of m — very common in optics and E&M.
    • Wrong formulaPower instead of current, force instead of energy.
    • Partial calculationStop one step short — rewards students who complete all steps.
    • Inverted ratioDivide where student should multiply.
    • Single-component errorUse one element instead of combined value in networks.
    Don't make the correct answer the only round number. All four choices should look equally clean at a glance.
    2.C
    Compare quantities across scenarios or over time

    MCQ: 10–15%
    In Situation 1, [setup A]. In Situation 2, [setup B]. How does [quantity] compare?
    • As [variable] increases from A to B, what happens to [quantity]?
    • Rank the following [quantities] from greatest to least.
    • What is the ratio of [X₁] to [X₂]?
    • Which correctly compares [X] and [Y]? (X>Y, X=Y, X<Y…)
    Ex 1 — Capacitor (battery disconnected)
    A capacitor is charged then disconnected. Plate separation is doubled from d to 2d. How does the electric field compare before and after?
    • (A) The fields are equal.
    • (B) Field before is twice as large.
    • (C) Field after is twice as large.
    • (D) Cannot be determined without knowing charge.
    Why each fails: Battery disconnected → Q fixed, not V. E = Q/Aε₀ is independent of d. (B) assumes V is constant — "battery still connected" error. (D) is a dodge — Q is implicitly fixed.
    Ex 2 — Photoelectric effect
    Light of frequency f hits Metal A (work function W) and ejects electrons with KE = K. Same light hits Metal B with work function 2W. How does K_B compare to K?
    • (A) K_B = 2K
    • (B) K_B = K
    • (C) K_B = K − W
    • (D) K_B = 0
    Why each fails: K = hf − W, so K_B = hf − 2W = K − W. (A) assumes KE doubles — backwards. (B) ignores work function difference. (D) assumes 2W always exceeds hf.
    • False cancellationTwo quantities both change — students assume they cancel but don't.
    • "They're equal" trapLooks symmetric but differs in what's held constant.
    • Hidden constraintBattery connected vs. disconnected; insulated vs. grounded. Richest source of 2.C distractors.
    • Wrong directionRight relationship, flipped comparison.
    • Intensive vs. extensivePressure vs. force, field vs. flux — scale differently.
    Setups should differ in exactly ONE variable for clean comparison. Two simultaneous changes are valid only if both are trackable.
    2.D
    Predict change using functional dependence

    MCQ: 10–15%
    If [variable] is [doubled/tripled/halved], by what factor does [quantity] change?
    • A student triples [X]. What happens to [Y]?
    • Which change to [variable] would cause [Y] to double?
    • The graph shows [X vs Y²]. If X increases by 4, Y becomes…
    • A new planet has [mass/radius] twice Earth's. How does [g] compare?
    Ex 1 — Coulomb's law (two changes)
    Two charges exert force F. Distance is tripled and one charge is doubled. By what factor does the force change?
    • (A) Factor of 2/3
    • (B) Decreases to 2F/9
    • (C) Decreases to 2F/3
    • (D) Increases by factor of 6
    Why each fails: F ∝ q₁q₂/r² → factor = 2/3² = 2/9. (A) forgets r is squared. (C) applies linear r. (D) multiplies both factors instead of dividing by r².
    Ex 2 — Thin lens
    A lens (focal length f) forms a real image at dₒ = 2f. Object moves to dₒ = 4f. How does image distance change?
    • (A) dᵢ doubles
    • (B) dᵢ decreases (from 2f to 4f/3)
    • (C) dᵢ stays the same
    • (D) No real image forms
    Why each fails: 1/dᵢ = 1/f − 1/dₒ. At 4f: dᵢ = 4f/3. (A) assumes image moves with object — intuitive but wrong. (D) is wrong because 4f > f.
    • Linear assumptionOffer linear result when relationship is quadratic — the single most common 2.D error.
    • Same-factor trapOutput changes by same factor as input. Always include this.
    • Wrong directionOffer value if quantity increased instead of decreased.
    • Single-change errorApply only one of two simultaneous changes correctly.
    • Square root vs. squareOffer both √n and n when answer is n².
    Limit stem to at most two simultaneously changing variables. One is cleanest; two is the ceiling for a fair MCQ.

    3.B
    Apply a law, model, or definition to make a claim

    MCQ: 20–25%
    Which of the following best explains why [phenomenon] occurs?
    • A student claims [X]. Is the student correct, and why?
    • Which correctly describes [behavior/outcome]?
    • Which law best supports the claim that [X]?
    • [Scenario]. Which correctly predicts what happens next?
    • A student argues [argument]. Which identifies the flaw?
    Ex 1 — Magnetic force direction
    A proton moves right through a region where B points into the page. A student claims the proton curves upward. Which best supports this claim?
    • (A) Field lines converge above, producing upward force.
    • (B) F = qv × B with rightward v and inward B yields upward force on positive charge.
    • (C) Proton is repelled by negative charges in the field region.
    • (D) Moving upward minimizes the proton's potential energy.
    Why each fails: (A) misapplies field line density. (C) fabricates charge accumulation. (D) uses an energy argument — magnetic forces do no work.
    Ex 2 — Kinetic theory (rigid container)
    An ideal gas in a rigid container is heated. A student claims pressure increases. Which best supports this claim using a microscopic model?
    • (A) Heating adds molecules, increasing collision frequency.
    • (B) Gas expands when heated, pushing harder on walls.
    • (C) Average kinetic energy increases, so wall collisions occur with greater force and frequency.
    • (D) Higher temperature causes walls to contract, reducing volume.
    Why each fails: (A) violates mass conservation. (B) contradicts "rigid." (D) invents wall contraction — tempting because students know P ∝ 1/V.
    • Correct conclusion, wrong reasoningRight outcome, physically incorrect explanation. Hardest distractor to eliminate — AP-authentic.
    • Documented misconception"Current is used up," "gravity doesn't act in space," "magnetic fields do work."
    • Irrelevant principleReal physics law that doesn't apply here.
    • Violated constraintChoice ignores a stated constraint (rigid, isolated, disconnected).
    • Cause-effect reversalFlip the causal direction of a relationship.
    All four choices must be grammatically parallel and similar in length. If the correct answer is noticeably longer, students identify it by elimination rather than physics.
    3.C
    Justify a claim with evidence from data or representations

    MCQ: 5–10%
    Which of the following [graphs / data / observations] best supports the claim that [physics claim]?
    • [Data table]. Which conclusion is best supported?
    • Which graph would be expected if [claim] is true?
    • A student observes [result]. Which claim is best supported?
    • The data below is most consistent with which model?
    Ex 1 — Ohm's Law (data table)
    A student doubles voltage each trial and finds current doubles proportionally. [Table provided.] Which claim is best supported?
    • (A) Resistance decreases as voltage increases.
    • (B) The resistor obeys Ohm's Law — constant resistance equals slope of V-vs-I graph.
    • (C) Current is independent of voltage.
    • (D) Power dissipated remains constant.
    Why each fails: (A) contradicts constant slope. (C) directly contradicts data. (D) is wrong because P = I²R increases with I.
    Ex 2 — Single-slit diffraction
    A student narrows a single slit and the central bright fringe widens. Which observation best supports the claim that light exhibits wave behavior?
    • (A) Central fringe narrows and brightens — consistent with light passing straight through.
    • (B) Central fringe widens and dims — consistent with greater diffraction through a smaller aperture.
    • (C) Pattern disappears — light cannot pass through narrow openings.
    • (D) Pattern shifts sideways — refraction at the slit edge.
    Why each fails: (A) describes particle behavior. (C) is nonphysical. (D) conflates diffraction with refraction.
    • Contradicts stimulusDirectly contradicts data — catches careless readers.
    • OvergeneralizationClaim goes beyond what the data can support.
    • Partial evidenceSupported by some rows of data but not all.
    • Axis misreadTrue if axes were swapped or slope confused with intercept.
    • Alternate modelConsistent with wrong physical model (particle vs. wave).
    • Correct claim, wrong evidenceTrue physics principle, but not demonstrated by this data.
    3.C questions require a stimulus (table, graph, or described observation) — they cannot stand alone as text. Design the stimulus first, then build distractors from its misreadings.
