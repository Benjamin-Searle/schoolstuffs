2.A MCQ: 15–20% Derive a symbolic expression from known quantities by selecting and following a logical mathematical pathway
Core stem pattern
Which of the following correctly expresses [unknown] in terms of [given variables]?
Variants
  • A student derives an expression for [X]. Which is correct?
  • Using only the quantities shown, which expression gives [X]?
  • Two students write different expressions for [X]. Which is valid, and why?
  • [Scenario + diagram]. Which of the following represents [X] in terms of [vars]?
Ex 1 — Electrostatics (potential energy)
A point charge Q is fixed at the origin. A test charge q is placed a distance r away. Which of the following correctly expresses the electric potential energy U of the test charge in terms of Q, q, r, and Coulomb's constant k?
  • (A) U = kQq / r
  • (B) U = kQq / r²
  • (C) U = kQ / r
  • (D) U = kQqr
Why each fails: (B) is the force formula F = kQq/r² — wrong quantity. (C) drops q — that's electric potential V, not energy. (D) inverts the r relationship — targets students who misremember the proportionality direction.
Ex 2 — Ideal gas law (isothermal)
A sealed container of ideal gas undergoes an isothermal compression from volume V₁ at pressure P₁ to a new pressure P₂. Which of the following correctly expresses the final volume V₂ in terms of P₁, V₁, and P₂?
  • (A) V₂ = P₂V₁ / P₁
  • (B) V₂ = P₁V₁ / P₂
  • (C) V₂ = P₁P₂ / V₁
  • (D) V₂ = V₁(P₁ + P₂)
Why each fails: (A) inverts the ratio — most common algebra error. (C) wrong units entirely, good for testing dimensional awareness. (D) replaces inverse proportion with addition — fundamental Boyle's Law misconception.
Distractor strategies
  • Ratio inversionSwap numerator/denominator (e.g., r vs. 1/r). Extremely common and hard to spot quickly.
  • Wrong-but-related equationUse the formula for a nearby quantity: F instead of U, V instead of U, E instead of F. Students half-recall the topic but grab the wrong expression.
  • Missing variableDrop one variable from the correct expression. Targets students who partially recall a formula.
  • Wrong exponentOff-by-one on a power: r instead of r², v instead of v². Very effective in Coulomb, gravity, and kinematics contexts.
  • Arithmetic shortcutReplace multiplication with addition or subtraction of variables (e.g., P₁ + P₂ instead of P₁/P₂).
  • Constant confusionSwap constants: k vs. G, or present 1/4πε₀ vs. k to test whether students recognize equivalence.
At least 2 of 3 wrong answers should be dimensionally consistent with the correct answer. A dimensionally obvious distractor is too easy to eliminate.
2.B MCQ: 20–25% Calculate or estimate an unknown quantity with units from known quantities
Core stem pattern
A [object/system] has [given numerical values with units]. What is the [unknown quantity]?
Variants
  • What is the approximate value of [X]? (order-of-magnitude estimate)
  • A student measures [givens]. Which of the following is closest to [X]?
  • Based on the diagram/data table, what is [X]?
  • What is the magnitude of [X]? (isolates magnitude from direction)
Ex 1 — Electric circuits (series)
A 12 V battery is connected to two resistors in series: R₁ = 4 Ω and R₂ = 8 Ω. What is the current through R₂?
  • (A) 0.75 A
  • (B) 1.5 A
  • (C) 1.0 A
  • (D) 3.0 A
Why each fails: (A) divides 12 V by only R₁ then halves — partial calculation. (B) uses only R₁ as the denominator. (D) uses only R₂. All three distractors reflect "use one resistor, not the sum" — the core series circuit misconception.
Ex 2 — Double-slit interference
Monochromatic light (λ = 600 nm) passes through two slits separated by d = 0.30 mm. The screen is L = 1.5 m away. What is the distance between adjacent bright fringes?
  • (A) 3.0 × 10⁻³ m
  • (B) 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ m
  • (C) 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ m
  • (D) 2.7 × 10⁻³ m
Why each fails: (B) inverts the formula: d/(λL). (C) omits L from the calculation. (D) uses λ = 600 mm instead of 600 nm — unit conversion error. All four answers are close in magnitude, preventing easy estimation.
Distractor strategies
  • Unit conversion errorGive nm instead of m, cm instead of m, μC instead of C — very common in optics and electrostatics. Use the "wrong units" value as a distractor.
  • Wrong formula selectionApply the formula for a related quantity: power instead of current, force instead of energy, period instead of frequency.
  • Partial calculationStop one step short of the final answer. Rewards students who complete all steps.
  • Inverted ratioDivide where the student should multiply, or flip numerator/denominator.
  • Single-component errorIn series/parallel networks, use only one element instead of the correct combined value.
  • Matched magnitudesKeep all four choices within one order of magnitude so estimation alone won't identify the answer.
Don't make the correct answer the only "round" number. If the correct answer is 3.0 × 10⁻³, all three distractors should also look clean at a glance.
2.C MCQ: 10–15% Compare physical quantities between two or more scenarios, or at different times/locations in a single scenario
Core stem pattern
In Situation 1, [setup A]. In Situation 2, [setup B]. How does [quantity] in Situation 1 compare to Situation 2?
Variants
  • As [variable] increases from point A to point B, what happens to [quantity]?
  • Rank the following [quantities] from greatest to least.
  • What is the ratio of [X₁] to [X₂]?
  • Which of the following correctly compares [X] and [Y]? (choices: X>Y, X=Y, X<Y, cannot be determined)
Ex 1 — Parallel plate capacitor (battery disconnected)
A parallel-plate capacitor is charged by a battery and then disconnected. In Situation 1, the plate separation is d. In Situation 2, the plates are pulled apart to 2d. How does the electric field between the plates in Situation 1 compare to Situation 2?
  • (A) The fields are equal in both situations.
  • (B) The field in Situation 1 is twice as large.
  • (C) The field in Situation 2 is twice as large.
  • (D) Cannot be determined without knowing the charge.
Why each fails: Battery disconnected → Q is constant (not V). E = σ/ε₀ = Q/Aε₀, which is independent of d. (B) assumes V is constant — the "battery still connected" error. (C) inverts the error. (D) is a dodge — charge is implicitly fixed.
Ex 2 — Photoelectric effect (comparing two metals)
Light of frequency f strikes Metal A (work function W), ejecting electrons with maximum kinetic energy K. The same light strikes Metal B, which has work function 2W. How does the maximum kinetic energy of electrons ejected from Metal B compare to K?
  • (A) K_B = 2K
  • (B) K_B = K
  • (C) K_B = K − W
  • (D) K_B = 0 (no electrons ejected)
Why each fails: Since K = hf − W, then K_B = hf − 2W = K − W. (A) assumes KE doubles with work function — backwards. (B) ignores work function difference. (D) is tempting if students assume 2W always exceeds hf, but K could still be positive if K > W.
Distractor strategies
  • False cancellationMake two quantities both change so students assume they cancel — but they don't cancel linearly. Works well with E&M (Q and d both change).
  • "They're equal" trapUse situations that look symmetric but differ in what's held constant. The "equal" option is either correct or the most tempting wrong answer.
  • Hidden constraintChange what's held constant between setups — battery connected vs. disconnected, insulated vs. grounded, rigid vs. flexible container. This is the richest source of 2.C distractors.
  • Wrong comparison directionStudents identify the right relationship but flip which scenario is larger.
  • "Cannot be determined" optionValid when a quantity is genuinely unknown, but also effective as a distractor when students over-apply uncertainty. Use sparingly.
  • Intensive vs. extensive confusionPressure vs. force, field vs. flux — these scale differently with size changes.
Both setups should differ in exactly ONE variable if you want a clean comparison. Changing two things at once is valid but must be intentional, as in Ex 2 above — and the stem must make the relationship trackable.
2.D MCQ: 10–15% Predict new values or factors of change using functional dependence between variables
Core stem pattern
If [variable] is [doubled / tripled / halved], by what factor does [dependent quantity] change?
Variants
  • A student triples [X]. What happens to [Y]? (factor-of-change)
  • Which change to [variable] would cause [Y] to double?
  • The graph shows [X] vs. [Y²]. If X is increased by a factor of 4, Y becomes…
  • A new planet has [mass/radius] twice that of Earth. How does [g/escape speed] compare?
Ex 1 — Coulomb's law (two variables change)
Two charges exert a force F on each other. If the distance between the charges is tripled and one charge is doubled, by what factor does the electric force change?
  • (A) Increases by a factor of 2/3
  • (B) Decreases by a factor of 9/2 (new force = 2F/9)
  • (C) Decreases by a factor of 3/2
  • (D) Increases by a factor of 6
Why each fails: F ∝ q₁q₂/r² → factor = 2/3² = 2/9. (A) forgets r is squared. (C) applies linear distance, not squared. (D) multiplies both factors instead of applying the inverse-square relationship — the most common error when two changes compound.
Ex 2 — Thin-lens equation (functional dependence)
A converging lens of focal length f forms a real image when an object is placed at distance dₒ = 2f. The object is then moved to dₒ = 4f. By what factor does the image distance dᵢ change?
  • (A) dᵢ doubles
  • (B) dᵢ stays the same
  • (C) dᵢ decreases (from 2f to 4f/3)
  • (D) No real image forms at dₒ = 4f
Why each fails: 1/dᵢ = 1/f − 1/dₒ. At dₒ=2f → dᵢ=2f; at dₒ=4f → dᵢ=4f/3. (A) assumes image moves the same direction as object — intuitive but wrong. (B) assumes thin-lens output is constant. (D) is wrong because 4f > f, so real image still forms.
Distractor strategies
  • Linear assumptionIf the relationship is quadratic (F ∝ 1/r²), offer the result as if it were linear (1/r). This is the single most common 2.D error.
  • Same-factor trapStudents assume the output changes by the same factor as the input — always offer this as a distractor.
  • Wrong directionOffer the value the quantity would take if it increased instead of decreased, and vice versa.
  • Single-change errorWhen two variables change, offer the result of applying only one of the changes correctly.
  • Square root vs. squareIn wave speed, Coulomb's law, gravity — offer both √n and n as distractors when the correct answer is n².
  • Inverse direction"Doubling distance halves force" (students ignore the square) — a distractor that gets the direction right but the magnitude wrong.
Limit each stem to at most two simultaneously changing variables. More than two makes the question a calculation puzzle rather than a test of functional reasoning. One change is cleanest; two is the ceiling.

3.B MCQ: 20–25% Apply an appropriate law, definition, theoretical relationship, or model to make a claim
Core stem pattern
Which of the following best explains why [phenomenon] occurs?
Variants
  • A student claims [X]. Is the student correct, and why?
  • Which of the following correctly describes [behavior/outcome]?
  • Which law or principle best supports the claim that [X]?
  • [Scenario]. Which of the following correctly predicts what happens next?
  • A student argues [argument]. Which statement identifies the flaw in the reasoning?
Ex 1 — Magnetism (force direction)
A proton moves horizontally to the right through a region where the magnetic field points into the page. A student claims the proton will curve upward. Which of the following best supports this claim?
  • (A) The field lines converge above the proton, producing a net upward force.
  • (B) Applying F = qv × B with rightward v and inward B gives an upward force on the positive charge.
  • (C) The proton is repelled by negative charges accumulating in the field region.
  • (D) Moving upward minimizes the proton's potential energy in the magnetic field.
Why each fails: (B) correctly applies the cross-product rule with the right-hand rule. (A) misapplies field line density. (C) fabricates charge accumulation. (D) uses an energy argument — magnetic forces do no work and create no potential energy.
Ex 2 — Thermodynamics (kinetic model)
An ideal gas in a rigid container is heated. A student claims the pressure increases. Which of the following best supports this claim using a microscopic model of the gas?
  • (A) Heating adds new molecules to the container, increasing collision frequency.
  • (B) The gas expands when heated, pushing harder on the walls.
  • (C) The average kinetic energy of the molecules increases, so collisions with the walls occur with greater force and frequency.
  • (D) Higher temperature causes the container walls to contract, reducing volume and raising pressure.
Why each fails: (C) correctly invokes kinetic molecular theory. (A) violates conservation of mass. (B) contradicts "rigid container." (D) introduces fictional wall contraction — tempting because students may recall P and V are inversely related.
Distractor strategies
  • Correct conclusion, wrong reasoningState the right outcome but give a physically incorrect explanation. Highly effective and AP-authentic — it's the hardest distractor to eliminate.
  • Documented misconceptionUse known student misconceptions: "current is used up," "gravity doesn't act in space," "magnetic fields do work," "a larger object falls faster."
  • Irrelevant principleInvoke a real physics law that doesn't apply here. Students who half-remember the topic grab it.
  • Energy red herringOffer an energy-based explanation for a force/field question where energy reasoning doesn't apply (e.g., magnetic force).
  • Violated constraintWrite a choice that ignores a stated constraint in the stem: rigid container, isolated system, battery disconnected.
  • Cause-effect reversalFlip the causal direction: "pressure increases because volume decreases" in a situation where volume is fixed.
All four answer choices must be grammatically parallel and similar in length. If the correct answer is noticeably longer or more detailed, students identify it by process of elimination rather than by physics reasoning.
3.C MCQ: 5–10% Justify or support a claim using evidence from experimental data, physical representations, or physical principles/laws
Core stem pattern
Which of the following [graphs / data / observations] best supports the claim that [physics claim]?
Variants
  • [Data table provided]. Which conclusion is best supported by this data?
  • Which graph would be expected if [claim] is true?
  • A student observes [result]. Which claim is best supported by this observation?
  • The data below is most consistent with which of the following models?
Ex 1 — Circuits (data stimulus)
A student measures the current (I) through a resistor at several voltages (V) and records the data in the table below. [Table: V doubles each row, I doubles proportionally.] Which of the following claims is best supported by this data?
  • (A) The resistance of the resistor decreases as voltage increases.
  • (B) The resistor obeys Ohm's Law, with a constant resistance equal to the slope of a V-vs-I graph.
  • (C) The current through the resistor is independent of the applied voltage.
  • (D) The power dissipated by the resistor remains constant as voltage changes.
Why each fails: (B) is best — linear V-I with constant slope means constant R. (A) contradicts constant slope. (C) directly contradicts the data. (D) is wrong because P = I²R — if I increases, P increases.
Ex 2 — Physical optics (observation stimulus)
A student shines monochromatic light through a single slit and observes a diffraction pattern on a distant screen. The slit width is then decreased. Which of the following observations best supports the claim that light exhibits wave behavior?
  • (A) The central bright fringe becomes narrower and more intense, consistent with light passing straight through.
  • (B) The central bright fringe becomes wider and the pattern spreads out, consistent with greater diffraction through a smaller aperture.
  • (C) The pattern disappears when the slit is narrow enough, showing that light cannot pass through small openings.
  • (D) The pattern shifts sideways, indicating refraction at the slit edge.
Why each fails: (B) explicitly links observation to the wave model. (A) describes particle behavior — beam narrows and concentrates. (C) is nonphysical. (D) conflates diffraction (wave spreading) with refraction (direction change at boundary).
Distractor strategies
  • Contradicts the stimulusInclude a choice that directly contradicts what the data or graph shows — catches students who don't read carefully or who pattern-match on a keyword.
  • OvergeneralizationOffer a claim that goes beyond what the data can support — correlation stated as causation, or a pattern from limited trials stated as a universal law.
  • Partial evidenceA choice supported by some rows of the data table but not all. Students who read quickly accept it.
  • Axis misreadOffer a claim that would be true if the student swapped the axes (slope vs. y-intercept, linear vs. inverse).
  • Alternate modelProvide a claim consistent with a wrong physical model (particle vs. wave, classical vs. quantum) that the observation superficially fits.
  • Correct claim, wrong evidenceA choice that states a true physics principle but one that's not actually demonstrated by this particular data or observation.
3.C questions require a stimulus — a data table, graph, diagram, or described observation. They cannot stand as plain-text questions. Design your stimulus first, then build distractors from common misreadings of it.
